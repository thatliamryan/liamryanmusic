ABOUT LIAM

Music is fun!

I'm a vocalist, musician, and music educator with a particular interest in helping people understand what's happening underneath the music they hear. I've always found theory most interesting when it stops being a collection of rules and starts becoming a way of describing something you can actually hear. A chord progression isn't just a series of Roman numerals. An interval isn't just a distance between two notes. Once you can connect the written idea to the sound, music theory becomes much more intuitive. That's the approach I bring into my lessons. My musical education began with performing. I spent years singing in choirs and ensembles before becoming increasingly interested in jazz, harmony, improvisation, and composition. I'm currently completing my graduate studies in jazz voice, where I've had the opportunity to study performance, arranging, composition, ear training, and advanced harmony. Working across these areas has shaped the way I think about theory. I don't see notation, harmony, ear training, and performance as separate subjects. They're different ways of understanding the same music. I've always enjoyed the moment when something that initially seems complicated suddenly clicks. Maybe a student has memorized the notes of a major scale but doesn't understand why they're arranged that way. Maybe they've learned that a dominant seventh chord "goes to" the tonic without understanding why it sounds like it wants to. Or maybe they've been given a page of intervals to memorize and can't connect any of them to what they actually hear. Those are the moments I enjoy teaching. Rather than simply giving students another rule to memorize, I try to help them understand why the rule exists and what it sounds like in practice. That might mean looking at notation, playing something at the piano, singing it, listening to an example, or working through an assignment together. The goal is always the same: turning an abstract concept into something you can hear, recognize, and use. There isn't one correct way to learn music theory. Some students are preparing for an exam and need a structured review of specific material. Others are returning to music after years away and want to rebuild their fundamentals. A songwriter might care much more about understanding harmony than learning how to identify a particular interval on a worksheet. That's why I don't expect every student to arrive with the same goals. If you have an assignment, bring it. If you have an upcoming exam, let's prepare for it. If there's a concept you've been trying to understand for months, let's figure it out. And if you aren't sure what you need help with yet, that's completely fine too. We'll figure it out together.
